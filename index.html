Note · News · November 2026

One story, seven headlines

When a major story breaks in Uganda, I find myself opening Daily Monitor, New Vision, NilePost, The Observer, Chimp Reports, Pulse and others — not because I distrust any one outlet, but because each reports a different part of the same story. One has the official statement. Another has eyewitness accounts. Another has the background. Another is first with a new development.

Following one story can mean jumping between websites, reading repeated headlines, and piecing together the full picture yourself. So I started building something to solve that problem for myself: take reporting from multiple trusted Ugandan news sources and present it as one evolving story — what sources agree on, what only one source is reporting, where reports differ, how the story develops over time, and the sources behind the information.

The goal isn’t to replace news organisations or decide which source is ‘right’. It’s to make it easier to understand the full picture.

I’m working on an MVP. If you’d like to try it when it’s ready, join the waitlist: https://lnkd.in/gN_i59cD — and here’s an example of how stories are currently presented: https://lnkd.in/gaKAcyRk

Honest feedback welcome — including if you think the idea is unnecessary.

Note · Deployment · November 2026

App Store vs Play Store: shipping the same app twice

This month I shipped RIZFM, a radio app, through the App Store. It was rejected first. The email said 'changes needed' and pointed me to a review page — the actual reason lived behind that link, not in the message. We fixed what was asked, resubmitted, and it went live two days later. Two more of our apps are still working through that same review queue.

Play Store taught me a different lesson earlier this year. An update to one of our apps was blocked — not for the code, but for the account. Google wanted an organization account, with our company verified against its D-U-N-S record. The old version stayed live; new releases waited on paperwork.

Same team, same code, two very different gates. Apple gates the build: review is strict, feedback is specific but buried, and every resubmission restarts a clock. Google gates the publisher: once the account is verified, releases move fast — but getting verified is its own project.

My takeaways so far:

  • Budget time for review, not just for building. A rejection is a schedule event, not a failure.
  • Read the reviewer's actual message before changing anything. Guessing wastes builds.
  • Sort your store accounts early — company verification, legal name matching your records exactly. It is boring until it blocks a release.
  • Keep builds precious. When your CI gives you a handful of free builds a month, every upload should be deliberate.

Shipping is part of the product. The store is not the finish line — it is another system to engineer around.

Note · WhatsApp · November 2026

WhatsApp Flows: forms inside the chat

Most people treat WhatsApp as a chat channel. I think that undersells it. With the WhatsApp Cloud API and WhatsApp Flows, the chat becomes an interface — structured screens inside the conversation where users fill forms, pick options, and confirm actions without leaving the app they already live in.

Regular chat breaks down fast for anything structured. 'Type your full name' becomes twenty variations, half of them wrong. A Flow fixes that: proper input fields, dropdowns, date pickers, validation at the field level, and clean structured data arriving at my backend instead of free text I have to parse and clean.

Where this shines is financial services:

  • Account opening — identity details, product selection, and confirmations as guided screens, with the data landing ready to process instead of typed into a chat thread.
  • Merchant payments — a merchant picks what they're paying for, confirms the amount, and gets a receipt in the same conversation.
  • Custom support — instead of 'how can I help?' followed by a long typed story, users pick their issue from structured options and give exactly the details support needs. Fewer back-and-forths, faster resolution.

The pattern I keep coming back to: chat for conversation, Flows for anything with fields. The user never installs anything new, never learns a new interface — and my systems receive data they can actually act on.

WhatsApp is where my users already are. Flows let me meet them there with real product, not just messages.

Note · Observability · November 2026

Sentry and PostHog: seeing what users actually hit

For the Kakebe shop app I wired in two tools that changed how I debug: Sentry for errors, PostHog for behaviour.

Sentry answers 'what broke?' Every crash and exception arrives with a stack trace, the device, the app version, and how many users hit it. Before Sentry, a bug report was 'the app closed when I tapped pay' — a guess dressed as a report. Now I see the exact line, how often it fires, and whether the fix I shipped actually stopped it. Release health alone is worth it: you can watch a bad build light up and roll back before users complain.

PostHog answers 'what did users do?' Events, funnels, session replay. I can see where people drop out of checkout, which screens get ignored, and replay a session to watch the exact tap sequence that confused someone. Feature flags mean I can ship something dark and turn it on for a few users first.

Together they close the loop: PostHog shows me where users struggle, Sentry shows me what broke while they struggled. One is the map, the other is the alarm.

My rules now: instrument from day one, not after the first production bug. Name events like you will read them in six months. And never let analytics become surveillance — collect what helps you fix the product, nothing more.

You cannot refine what you cannot see. These two tools are how I see.

Note · Building

Spec-driven building

Keeping a spec as the single source of truth while an app is built screen by screen. When the spec and the build disagree, stop and decide — don’t let drift decide for you.

Note · Quality

Verification over generation

Generating code is cheap now. Checking a build against its spec — screen by screen, number by number — is the work that makes it done.

Note · Money

Money pacing, in one division

Most budgeting fails on pacing, not totals. Take what’s left after bills, divide by the days left in the month. That one number, checked daily, changes behaviour.

Note · Docs · November 2026

Documentation should follow implementation

Docs that describe features you haven’t built yet are fiction, and fiction in a README costs everyone time. When I wrote up the RIZFM API, I checked every documented endpoint against the running service — if it wasn’t implemented, it didn’t go in the docs. Write the docs after the code works, then keep them honest every time the code changes.

Note · Debugging · November 2026

Don’t hide upstream failures

When a service upstream returns a precise error — say a 422 saying exactly which field it rejected — and your service swallows it and reports a generic 500, you’ve deleted the only useful information in the system. I’ve lost hours to that pattern. Preserve the real error: pass it through, log it verbatim, surface it. A vague failure is a bug in its own right.

Note · Architecture · November 2026

Keep simple systems simple

The RIZFM API runs on Flask, SQLite, Gunicorn and Docker — because that’s all a station directory with health checks actually needs. No message broker, no microservices, no Kubernetes. Every layer you add is a layer you maintain, monitor and debug at 2 AM. Earn complexity; don’t start with it.

Note · Production · November 2026

Health checks are part of the product

“It compiles” is not “it works”. A /health endpoint, stream checks that catch a dead radio feed before a listener does, a certificate expiry on someone’s calendar, a queue you can see backing up — that’s production reality. If you can’t tell whether the system is alive without SSH-ing in and guessing, the system isn’t finished.

Note · Systems · November 2026

Queues over blocking

A webhook that does all its downstream work inline is a crash waiting for traffic. When hundreds of requests land at once, the handler should acknowledge fast, put the work on a queue, and let workers drain it at a pace the downstream systems can survive. Decouple first; your future on-call self will thank you.
